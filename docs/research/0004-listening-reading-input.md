## 1. Research Question

What are the psycholinguistic mechanisms of listening and reading comprehension for adult L2 learners, and how should comprehensible input ($i+1$), acoustic segmentation, connected speech, and audio-assisted reading be operationalized in a 60-minute daily routine?

---

## 2. Theoretical Foundations

### 2.1 The Comprehensible Input Hypothesis ($i+1$) Under Modern Cognitive SLA
Stephen Krashen's Input Hypothesis (1982, 1985) asserted that language is acquired solely through understanding messages containing structures slightly beyond the learner's current competence ($i+1$).
Modern cognitive SLA (VanPatten, 2004; Doughty & Long, 2003; Schmidt, 1990) refines this premise:
- **Input is Necessary but Not Sufficient**: Comprehensible input provides the essential raw linguistic data for internal hypothesis formulation.
- **The Noticing Hypothesis (Schmidt, 1990)**: Input does not become **intake** unless the learner consciously notices the target linguistic features in the input.
- **Input Processing Theory (VanPatten, 2004)**: Learners process input for meaning before form. Unless prompted or scaffolded, learners prioritize content words (nouns/verbs) and completely overlook grammatical inflections (e.g., third-person *-s*, past tense *-ed*).
- **The $i+1$ Operational Target**: Target approximate lexical coverage of ~95% for scaffolded input, modulated by domain schema and visual/audio support. While not a rigid biological cliff, unassisted input with low lexical coverage risks cognitive overload, excessive guesswork, and fatigue.

### 2.2 Psycholinguistics of L2 Listening: Bottom-Up vs. Top-Down Processing
John Field (2008, *Listening in the Language Classroom*) and Vandergrift & Goh (2012) elucidate why listening is typically the most anxiety-inducing and difficult skill for adult beginners:
- **Top-Down Processing**: Utilizing background knowledge, situational context, and topic schemas to infer meaning. Beginners over-rely on top-down guessing because their bottom-up decoding is too slow.
- **Bottom-Up Processing**: Acoustic signal decoding $\rightarrow$ phoneme discrimination $\rightarrow$ word boundary identification (segmentation) $\rightarrow$ syntactic parsing $\rightarrow$ proposition building.
- **The Acoustic Segmentation Crisis**: In spoken English, words are not separated by physical silences. Due to **connected speech** phenomena, the acoustic signal of *"What are you going to do?"* is realized as [wʌtʃə gənə duː]. An adult learner whose internal representations are based purely on written text fails to recognize words they theoretically "know".

### 2.3 Connected Speech Phenomena in American English
Spoken General American English (GAE) alters word boundaries systematically:
1. **Flapping / Tapping**: Intervocalic alveolar stops /t/ and /d/ become an alveolar tap [ɾ] when following a stressed syllable (e.g., *water* [ˈwɑːɾɚ], *get it* [ˈɡɛɾɪt]).
2. **Elision**: Deletion of unstressed vowels or weak consonants (e.g., *last night* [læs naɪt], *camera* [ˈkæmrə]).
3. **Assimilation**: Sound change conditioned by adjacent phonemes (e.g., *good boy* [gʊb bɔɪ], *did you* [ˈdɪdʒuː]).
4. **Vowel Reduction & Weak Forms**: Unstressed grammatical function words (*to, for, can, and, of*) reduce to schwa /ə/ (e.g., *can* /kæn/ becomes /kən/; *for* /fɔːr/ becomes /fɚ/).
5. **Linking / Liaison**: Consonant-to-vowel linking (*hold on* [hoʊl-dɑːn]) and vowel-to-vowel linking via intrusive glides [j, w] (*go out* [goʊ-waʊt]).

## 3. Evidence-Based Listening Techniques

| Technique | Description & Cognitive Mechanism | Evidence Strength | Optimal Level | Minutes / Role in 60m Routine |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bimodal Input (Reading-While-Listening)** | Listening to natural audio while tracking the synchronized written text. Bridges phoneme-grapheme correspondences (Chang, 2011). | `STRONG EVIDENCE` | Pre-A1 to B1 | 15–20 min (Core input block) |
| **Narrow Listening** | Listening to multiple short, authentic recordings by different speakers on the exact same narrow topic. Re-exposes learner to identical lexical frames with phonetic variability (Krashen, 1996). | `MODERATE EVIDENCE` | A2 to B2 | 10–15 min |
| **Micro-Dictation (Bottom-Up Decoding)** | Transcribing short 3–7 word spoken audio clips featuring connected speech. Forces conscious noticing of weak forms, articles, and prepositions (Field, 2008). | `STRONG EVIDENCE` | A1 to B1 | 5–10 min (Focused drill) |
| **Speech-Rate Scaffolding** | Slowing audio slightly (0.85x–0.9x) with pitch preservation, gradually transitioning to natural speed (1.0x). Prevents working memory overflow during initial parsing (Zhao, 1997). | `MODERATE EVIDENCE` | A0 to A2 | Early scaffold only; must transition to 1.0x quickly |
| **Shadowing** | Rapidly repeating spoken audio aloud with minimal delay (250–500ms). Automates articulatory muscle memory and prosodic entrainment (Kadota, 2019). | `MODERATE EVIDENCE` | A2 to B2 | 5–10 min (Careful: excessive cognitive load for absolute A0) |

## 4. Reading Development: Extensive vs. Intensive

### 4.1 Extensive Reading (ER)
- **Principle**: Reading large quantities of easy, enjoyable material (graded readers) at $\ge 98\%$ lexical coverage without stopping for dictionary lookups (Day & Bamford, 1998; Nation & Macalister, 2020).
- **Outcomes**: Builds reading fluency, sight-word recognition speed, grammatical intuition, and incidental consolidation of previously primed vocabulary.
- **Graded Readers**: Essential tool for A1–B1 learners. A graded reader uses a strictly controlled vocabulary headword count (e.g., 300-word, 600-word, 1,200-word levels) to guarantee $\ge 98\%$ coverage.

### 4.2 Intensive Reading (IR)
- **Principle**: Short, dense texts (100–300 words) analyzed deeply for syntax, grammar patterns, collocations, and unfamiliar vocabulary.
- **Outcomes**: Form-focused noticing, sentence mining for Anki cards, and explicit structural analysis.

## 5. Architectural Implications for English Learning OS

1. **No Raw Unscaffolded Audio for Beginners**: Audio presented to A0–A1 learners must be accompanied by synchronized transcripts or pedagogical captions.
2. **Connected Speech Explicit Instruction**: The curriculum must explicitly teach GAE connected speech rules (flapping, reductions, weak forms) early to prevent acoustic alienation.
3. **Graded Reader Integration**: Reading assignments during Months 1–6 should utilize calibrated Graded Readers or scaffolded texts targeting high lexical accessibility (~95% coverage target), supplemented by contextual glosses.
4. **Bimodal Default**: Default mode for receptive study is **Reading-While-Listening** (audio paired with text), transitioning to pure audio-only listening after the text has been decoded.
